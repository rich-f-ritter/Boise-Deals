## I. The Boise Economy — seven need-to-knows

**1. The published job number understates reality; the tax records say Boise is still growing ~2.4%.** CES payroll growth prints +0.42% T3M YoY (Jun-26) — but QCEW hard counts (UI tax records, ~97% of jobs) show **Ada +2.51% / Canyon +2.09% YoY in Q4-25** vs CES +0.91% for the same window. The divergence opened in the shutdown quarter, hit 9 of 10 peer metros, and was worst in the fastest-growing metros (the CES birth-death model's signature failure). Sector hard counts: healthcare **+7.1%**, construction **+6.7%**, hospitality +2.0% — the sectors the survey shows collapsing — while genuine softness sits in the white-collar office economy (finance −1.8%, prof services −1.5%, mfg −1.4%: the HP-exit / Albertsons-erosion story). Boise's CES re-benchmarks to the tax records in early 2027; expected direction is up. *[Research paper §1–2; appendices E, J; BLS data re-verified 8/13/26]*

**2. The real slowdown is national — a labor-supply event, not a Boise demand failure.** Shift-share: Boise's local outperformance went from +7,869 jobs/yr to +673; Boise still beats the nation in 7 of 10 sectors — the nation simply stalled (US 2025: +181K payrolls *for the year*; first negative net US migration in ~50 years; break-even payroll growth ≈ 0 per Dallas/StL/KC Fed). *[Paper §3; appendix G]*

**3. Local softness is specific and mostly policy/weather:** Idaho tax cuts → deficit → 4% agency cuts (state gov jobs −7.4% YoY, federal −5.0%); leisure hit by closures + the worst ski winter in years — not tourism (BOI's 4th straight record year); healthcare hiring paused (Blue Cross Medicaid contract loss; small-practice closures) even as hospital postings run +44%. *[Appendix H]*

**4. The labor market is supply-constrained — rent-supportive, not demand-broken:** Idaho wage growth 3rd-fastest in the US (+4.7%); SW Idaho job postings +11% (semiconductors +92%); unemployment 3.4% vs US 4.4%. The measured labor-force decline (−0.79% — *people*, not jobs) is largely population-control lag plus migrant composition (retirees/remote workers who rent without joining local payrolls). *[Appendices A, H; paper §5]*

**5. Migration re-accelerated to a five-year high — and it's landing on the subject.** Placer device data (through Jun-26, 11 months ahead of Census): net domestic migration **~+11.6–12.4K YE Jun-26, ~2× the prior year** (client-confirmed: the +8.5K O-D table sum is not the level); **Boise = #5 of 925 US CBSAs (+1.45% rate); Ada = #8 of 3,126 US counties**; California = 47% of net inflow; the Washington-DC feeder doubled (federal exodus, $127K feeder MHHI). **ZIP 83642 — the subject's ZIP — is the metro's #1 in-migration ZIP (+2,417, +34.7/1,000, accelerating +1,077 YoY)** while north Meridian decelerated −511: the flow is rotating into the subject's corridor. *[Appendix K; data/placerai/]*

**6. Housing math does the demand work payrolls can't:** own-vs-rent spread ~$1,500–1,900/mo (median Meridian house ~$550K at 6–7% mortgage rates vs ~$1,700–1,800 rents); metro construction pipeline collapsed to ~600 units under construction (−55% YoY; completions −64%); absorption ~2,500+/yr; occupancy 94.3%. *[Appendices D, F; CBRE/Lee/MMG]*

**7. The 2027–2030 catalyst stack is dated and visible — so are the risks.** Catalysts: Micron ID1 first DRAM output mid-2027 (ID2 2028; ~2,000 direct jobs ramping; semiconductor postings already +92%); ~$1.55B of hospital openings 2028–30 (St. Luke's Boise tower; Saint Alphonsus Nampa + North Meridian); District at Ten Mile Phase 1 spring-2027 (Target, Life Time, In-N-Out); SH-16 freeway and Gowen F-16 in 2027; $700M airport program. Risks: HP's full Boise exit by end-2027 (~1.1–1.7K high-income jobs), Albertsons HQ erosion, Blue Cross selling its Meridian campus (802 jobs), Medicaid cuts landing 2027, Micron memory-cycle risk, and Ten Mile-corridor supply resuming 2028+ (Southridge 640 u/c is the closest competitor; Vanguard Village's 552 entitled units = the permit tripwire). *[Paper §7; appendices B, C, H; District dossier]*

**Underwriting posture:** base +1.0–1.5%/yr metro employment growth (+2.0–2.5% upside if the Sept-2026 QCEW confirms; ~0% stress — which the market already absorbed in H2-2025 at 94%+ occupancy). Demand is underwritten off migration + own-vs-rent + the supply trough more than payrolls. **Checkpoints: QCEW Q1-26 (~Sept 2026) · Census Vintage 2026 (Dec-26–Mar-27) · CES benchmark (early 2027).**

## II. The Seasons at Meridian Tenant Base — seven need-to-knows

*(Rent roll 7/9/26: 342 of 360 occupied = 95.0%, 38 on notice, 18 vacant. Demographic file covers 305 units / 613 occupants. First-turn ledger: 314-resident initial cohort.)*

**1. Micron is 2% of the tenant base — the "what else besides Micron" question is answered inside the rent roll.** Micron-direct employees: **9 residents = 2.0% of employed occupants**; Micron-related on the broadest definition (direct + fab construction subs + eng/design + semi vendors): **28 = 6.3% of employed; 24 units = 7.9%**. (Context: Canyon Ridge, the 2024-vintage lease-up adjacent to the Micron campus, runs ~71% Micron-related — Seasons is the structural opposite.) Seasons' exposure to a Micron shock is de minimis on the tenant side; Micron's 2027–28 ramp is upside it doesn't depend on. *[tenant-base/Demographics_Comparison.xlsx, Tier Summary]*

**2. Employer diversification is about as good as multifamily gets:** 366 distinct employers across 480 reporting occupants; **largest single employer = St. Luke's at 4.0%** (19 residents); top-5 = 12.4%; top-10 = 18.5%. The roster reads like the subject's own corridor: St. Luke's 19, Saint Alphonsus 13, Micron 10, Amazon 7, Albertsons 5, Chick-fil-A 5, ICCU 4, West Ada School District 4, State of Idaho 4 — the location premium is literally visible in the tenant list. *[tenant-base/Seasons_at_Meridian_Demographic_Analysis.xlsx, Employers]*

**3. The industry mix mirrors the metro's QCEW-verified growth engines:** healthcare #1 at **19.3% of employed occupants** (median income $55.2K) — the same sector growing +7.1% in Ada County hard counts — then construction/trades 13.0% ($58.9K), food service 9.2% ($33.4K), tech/semiconductors 8.4% ($58.5K), professional services 8.4% ($66.0K), government 7.5% ($69.3K). Defensive sectors = 26.8% of occupants; cyclical = 41.6%. *[Demographic Analysis, Industry Mix]*

**4. This is a young, roommate-heavy, pre-family renter base — the exact cohort the own-vs-rent gap traps in rentals:** median occupant age **27.5 vs city 37.5**; **51% of occupants are 18–29**; households with minors just 12.8%; single-occupant 33%, two-person 44%; roommate households 34% of units; guarantor-backed 9.5%; 1.24 vehicles/unit; 36% pet penetration; avg household size 2.01 vs Meridian ~2.7. *[Demographic Analysis, Summary/Age/HH Composition]*

**5. Incomes are workforce-solid, not luxury:** median household income **$80–89K** (reported vs screened basis) — ~40% above the Meridian renter median ($61.9K), below the city all-household median ($100.8K); median personal income $58.0K; half of households are dual-earner. Rent coverage: median **3.84×**; **79% of units ≥3.0×**; thin tail of **8.2% below 2.0×**. At the median, rent-to-income is ~22%; the affordability stress table shows +10% rents still leave ~68% of households under a 30% burden. *[Demographic Analysis, HH Income/Affordability; Demographics_Comparison, Income & Coverage]*

**6. The first turn is churn-heavy but economically productive — concession burn-off is the engine.** Of the 314-resident initial cohort, 154 expirations had come due by 8/4/26: **64 renewed (41.6% retention; 46.1% counting month-to-month holdovers)**, 83 moved out, plus 21 early terminations (all-in retention ~37%). But the economics of the turn are strongly positive because **71% of initial leases carried a concession averaging $2,058 (~1.2 months free)** that disappears at turn: renewal trade-outs are +1.5% gross but **+9.5% effective**; new-lease trade-outs +5.2% gross / **+10.7% effective**, with an average 23 days vacant between tenants. Renewal concessions collapse to ~$23; replacement-lease concessions ~$456. Retention by floor plan: 1×1 44%, 2×2 40%, 3×2 38% (A2 units weakest at 21%; B1 best at 60%). 139 cohort leases haven't hit first expiration yet — the first turn is roughly halfway done. *[tenant-base/Seasons_at_Meridian_First_Turn_Analysis.xlsx]*

**7. Current-state flags to carry honestly:** 38 units on notice (11.1% of occupied) and July-2026 retention printed weak (8 of 37); the largest resident cohort (18–24, 26% of occupants, median income $38.4K) is the most churn- and payment-sensitive; contract rents (median $1,714) sit ~11% below the property's own market rents — which the first-turn data shows is being *captured* at turn via concession burn-off, at the cost of elevated churn and ~23 days of vacancy per turn. Data-quality caveats per the analysis workbook (cartesian-join inflation, 23 monthly-keyed salaries, ~21% blank employers) are handled via the screened basis. *[First Turn Analysis; Demographic Analysis, Data Quality]*

## III. Synthesis

The tenant file independently validates the market research, and vice versa. The metro's *actual* (tax-record-verified) growth sectors — healthcare, construction, logistics — are the tenant base's three largest industries; the corridor's anchor employers appear by name in the roster; the asset sits in the metro's #1 in-migration ZIP in a metro that just posted a top-5-national migration year. Micron — the headline question — is 2% of the tenant base directly: Seasons is a bet on the diversified Meridian workforce economy, with Micron's 2027–28 ramp as unpriced upside. The demand engine is the young, priced-out-of-ownership renter (51% of occupants aged 18–29, facing a $1,500–1,900/mo own-vs-rent gap), and both datasets say that engine re-accelerated through mid-2026 while metro supply collapsed. The honest watch items: first-turn retention of ~42% with 23-day turn vacancy (productive churn, but an occupancy-management load), the 8.2% thin-coverage tail, white-collar erosion touching the top of the renter income distribution, and Ten Mile-corridor supply resuming 2028+.
